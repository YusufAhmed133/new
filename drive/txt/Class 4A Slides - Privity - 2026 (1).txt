                             Privity of contract



• What is privity of contract?

Rule: only a person who is a party to a contract can enforce the
contract and incur obligations under it:
• Consequently -
• A contract cannot confer an enforceable benefit on a non-
  party
• Nor can it impose an enforceable burden on a non-party.
                                      Privity of contract

How might a non-party get relief despite the privity rule?
1. The contracting party might enforce the contract (Beswick v. Beswick (1966) Ch
    538, at p 554 Lord Denning M.R)
2. Maybe argue the privity rule does not apply (C was a party to the
   contract between A and B via agency)
3. C was acting as a trustee for one party to the contract between A and B
4. C establishes an estoppel against B if B has led C to believe:
   1.   C would have enforceable rights against B; or
   2.   B would perform a certain promise
        and C has relied on that assumption and would suffer detriment if
B acted inconsistently with the assumption
5. C establishes that B’s conduct in making the contract was misleading or
    deceptive in breach of the CCA

                        Circumventing the Privity rule
Trident General Insurance Co Ltd v McNiece Bros Pty Ltd
                              (1988) 165 CLR 107
13 June 1977: Blue Circle Southern Cement Ltd contracts with Trident General
Insurance to cover any expenses concerning their subsidiaries, contractors
and suppliers (this includes any personal injury matters).
Blue Circle, and its subcontractors, are now entitled to indemnity under
Trident’s Insurance Policy.
McNiece Bros subsequently becomes the principal contractor for a
construction work plant owned by Blue Circle.
4 July 1979: A crane driver (employee of another company) who was working under
direction of McNiece was injured on site.
The crane driver was successfully awarded compensation on grounds of
personal injury against McNiece.
McNiece sought indemnity (compensatory relief) from Trident General
Insurance.
Trident denies the claim based upon the grounds of doctrine of privity as
McNiece did not have a direct contract with them.

Mason CJ + Wilson J (joint judgement):
● Both open to the idea of reconsidering the strict application of
  the Doctrine of Privity, just not in this case (as it deals with the
  matter of insurance) - they question whether the “old rules
  should apply”

 ● Mason CJ and Wilson J stated the privity rule had been eroded
   by statute and had the potential to cause injustice, saying:
   “…we consider it is the responsibility of this court to reconsider
   in appropriate cases common law rules which operate
   unsatisfactorily and unjustly.”
Mason CJ and Wilson J

“It stands to reason that many subcontractors will assume that such an
insurance is an effective indemnity in their favour and that they will
refrain from making their own arrangements for insurance on that
footing.
That, it seems, is what happened in the present case.
In the circumstances, notwithstanding the caution with which the court
ordinarily will review earlier authorities and the operation of long-
established principle, we conclude that the principled development of the
law requires that it be recognised that McNiece was entitled to succeed
in the action.”

                            Pros of retaining the Privity Doctrine


Practical considerations
1.   If both the promisee and the 3rd party could enforce a promise by
     promisor, then double recovery would be possible
2.   Privity protects a promisor from exposure to the risk of many potential
     plaintiffs
3.   Entitlement of a 3rd party to enforce a contract might constrain the
     freedom of action of promisor and promisee
Theoretical considerations
1.   A 3rd party who is promised a benefit under a contract has not
     participated in the bargain and has not paid for the promise
2.   Recognising 3rd party rights gives public interests priority over private
     interests
                             Cons of the Privity Doctrine

1.   Thwarts intentions of contracting parties
2.   May cause injustice to 3rd parties
3.   Prevents 3rd party who has suffered loss from suing – but party who has
     suffered no loss may sue
4.   Availability of remedies depends on promisee’s willingness and ability to
     sue (eg: Coulls v Bagot)
5.   The range of CL and statutory circumventions suggest the rule is unjust
6.   The range of exceptions makes the law complex, artificial and uncertain
7.   A 3rd party can acquire rights under a contract in civil law jurisdictions and
     via legislation in numerous CL jurisdictions
8.   JJ recognise the inconvenience and injustice of the rule
9.   Privity causes difficulties for parties to commercial contracts who want to
     confer benefits on 3rd parties.
