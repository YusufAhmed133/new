                                     Bargains or Conditional Gifts


Australian Woollen Mills Pty Ltd v Commonwealth
Example 1 – No request

➢ A said to B: “I will pay you £1000 on your arrival in Sydney”: no contract as there may be no relation
  whatever between A’s statements and B’s act. It is consistent with the facts proved that B intended to go to
  Sydney anyhow, and that A is merely announcing that, if and when B arrives in Sydney, he will make a GIFT
  to him.

➢ The necessary relation is not shown to exist between the announcement and the act

Example 2 – Additional facts

➢ A had told B, before the mentioning of the money, that it was a matter of vital important to A that B should
  come to Sydney; B objected that to go to Sydney at the moment might involve him in financial loss. B goes
  to Sydney because of the money offered.

➢ This suggests:

➢ (1) the statement to pay the money was intended as an offer of a promise;

➢ (2) the promise was offered as the consideration for the doing of the act by B;

➢ (3) the doing of the act was at once the acceptance of the offer and the providing of an executed
  consideration for a promise

                         Beaton v McDivitt

Facts:
• McDivitts (respondents) owned 25 acres. Believed local council planned to
   rezone – council rates would increase – McD would not be able to afford
   rates – decided to subdivide into 4 lots – keep 3, let 4th to someone for
   permaculture – transfer when rezoned
• Beatons (appellant) moved to 4th block, farmed using permaculture
• 7 years on – dispute
• McD told B to leave
Held in Supreme Ct (Young J):
• Beatons had not given consideration sufficient to satisfy the bargain
   requirement of something given in exchange for the promise.
• But acknowledged Dillwyn v Llewelyn exception that Beaton’s acts of
   reliance gave rise to a contract
• But that contact had been frustrated.

Beaton appealed to SCt CA (Kirby P, McHugh JA, Mahoney JA)
                                                                           12
                         Beaton v McDivitt (appeal)

Kirby P:
• Without consideration, a promise is unenforceable at law.
• The expenditures and actions of [Beaton] are properly categorised
   as entirely for his own benefit.
   – ie – not as consideration
• Why should the law hold a party to the promise of a gift unless it
  can be demonstrated that, in return for the promise, the promisor
  receives some bargain from the promisee by way of a quid pro quo?
• Was Beaton’s NYE promise to maintain the private road to the
  property sufficient quid pro quo to be consideration?
   – No.
• Beaton was a bare licensee, no enforceable contract, no equity to
  force a transfer

                                                                      13
                           Beaton v McDivitt (appeal)

McHugh JA:
• Agrees with Young J’s finding a contract existed
• Disagrees with its terms
• McDivitt made a promise he intended to be binding (offer)
• 3 possible scenarios:
     – A promise to transfer property subject to an event occurring – bare
        promise, not enforceable
     – Promise to transfer property, after which the promisee acts to his own
        detriment – outcome depends on facts and equity
     – Promise contains a request by promisor to do an act/ fulfil a condition –
        usually doing the act/performing the condition = consideration
•   Frustration argument failed
•   McD have contractual duty to do everything they can to provide B with benefit
    of the contract

                                                                               14
                      Beaton v McDivitt (appeal)

Mahoney JA:
• There was a binding contract but no obligation on McD to pursue
  rezoning
• Focussed on the prospective rezoning as a condition subsequent
   – No time specified for rezoning
   – Rezoning was not a term of the contract
• Frustration to be tested by the circumstances
   – ‘But what if the rezoning doesn’t occur? In 1977 both McD and B
     would have answered – of course, then the land won’t be
     transferred’

Conclusion: Appeal dismissed

Three questions call for decision by their Lordships' Board.

(1) Does the guarantee upon its proper construction state a valid con
    sideration for the defendants' promise of indemnity?

(2) Does the extrinsic evidence as to the circumstances in which the
guarantee was given establish the existence of a valid consideration
additional to the consideration, if any, stated in the agreement?

(3) If there be consideration for the promise of indemnity, is the
guarantee nevertheless unenforceable, the consent of the defendants
having been induced by duress? Upon the third question there are
concurrent findings of fact. It would have to be shown, therefore, that
the judges below had misconceived the relevant law before the Board
could reverse their unanimous rejection of the defendants' case based
on economic duress.
