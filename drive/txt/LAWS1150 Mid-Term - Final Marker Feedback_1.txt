4. Is this the objectively best answer?

No. It is a very strong answer, and on the authorities it uses it is almost faultless: every quotation checks verbatim, every pin is right,
every judge is correctly named, the one dissent is labelled, the one reversed case is flagged, and the single-judge dictum is hedged.
It is not the best answer for four reasons.

First, C1 is a substantive error, not a slip. The problem proves that Rocky provided a guarantee; the live question is what he
provided. By saying AMPL’s call “does not prove” his assent, the answer frames Rocky’s exposure on a premise the facts deny, and a

satisfactory conditions’; Jessel MR’s words quoted at Masters 362 deal with an agreement “subject to certain conditions then

best case.

Third, it leaves four arguments unmet that a contracts academic would expect for one side or the other: privity of the $10,000
promise (K7), the duty to co-operate against AMPL’s premature call (K10), the third-party branch of the existing legal duty rule (K9),
and whether Rocky’s own ‘ensure’ undertaking is promissory or mere comfort (K8).

Fourth, two citations are used for less than they say (K3, K6).

The better answer would: fix C1 to C3; put Crossley (through Masters 362) against Grace on the qualifier; add the one-sentence

2. Contestable points, with fixes

  1. Part I is framed around Grace’s claim, not Rocky’s. The question asks for the ‘contractual claims he could make’. The
     opening says the primary issue is whether ‘Grace could claim that Rocky failed to lend’. The claim Rocky would actually make
     against Grace under the February dealings is that AMPL’s advance, made at his request, performed his 12 February promise,
     so that Grace owes him repayment. The answer dismisses this in a clause (‘neither performed his email’). Further analysis is
     required as your statement is conclusive. The fix is to frame the opening on Rocky’s claim (word-neutral). Then give one
     sentence of reasons: the advance was $1,150,000 rather than $1 million, made on 27 February rather than ‘by 15 February’,
     and in AMPL’s name, with terms first fixed at 4% on 8 April (about +25 words).
  2. The Lord Reid conditions are used to decide whether Pierre contracted for AMPL (fn 22: ‘The same agency conditions
     guide whether Pierre … contracted on its behalf’). Lord Reid’s four requisites govern whether a carrier contracted as agent for
     a stevedore so that the stevedore could take the benefit of exemption clauses. Requisites (1) and (4), that the bill makes the
     protection clear and that consideration moves from the stevedore, have no counterpart in whether a director’s oral promise
     binds his company. The better course is to frame the question as which party Rocky’s ‘you’ objectively addressed (Ermogenous
     [25]), with the authority and purporting facts as the answer already weighs them. The ‘further information’ about whom Pierre
     spoke for should also be named (R6).
  3. Port Jackson is cited at 269 (Mason and Jacobs JJ), the majority that the Privy Council reversed. The ‘rev’d’ history is
     disclosed, which is correct AGLC practice. However, the sheet prefers 240 (Barwick CJ, who would have upheld the stevedore’s
     protection), and the reversal invites a marker to query the source. Since the requisites themselves were never doubted, either
     pin is defensible.
  4. Footnote 4 (consideration) rests on McHugh JA’s description of ‘classical theory’, where ‘the typical contract is a
     bilateral one and consists of an exchange of promises’ (Brambles 177). That passage is about the limits of offer-and-acceptance
     analysis, not a statement of consideration doctrine. Australian Woollen Mills 456–7 states the bargain (quid pro quo) principle
     directly and is the better footnote here, unlike at fn 31.
  5. II.C2 misses Pao On’s third-party point. If Pierre promised personally, any existing guarantee duty was owed to AMPL, not
     to Pierre. A promise to perform a duty owed to a third party is good consideration (Pao On 632), and Mason J’s own limit,
     ‘made to the promisee under that contract’ (Wigan 594), already points there. This is the strongest answer to AMPL’s or
     Pierre’s existing legal duty argument, and it costs one clause (+15).
  6. II.D omits the argument that most helps Rocky on his own exposure. The answer finds intention ‘whoever promised’
     because a fixed sum is ‘appropriately promissory’. It does not put the opposite point for Rocky’s own undertaking: that ‘ensure’
     is closer to the comfort given in Banque Brussels, where the letter of comfort conditioned the facility. Only after that is met
     should it be found ‘weaker here’ because Rocky’s words were given for a price. As it stands, Banque Brussels is cited without
     saying what the case was about (+20).
  7. II.H omits the duty to co-operate. Rocky’s best answer to ‘once Grace repays’ is not only a commercial reading (Upper
     Hunter 437). He can also argue that AMPL may not rely on a condition whose fulfilment its own call frustrates. That argument
     should be put and met: the call does not stop Grace repaying (R-O), so it fails (+20).
  8. II.I4 omits the March instalment. Interest is shown paid only ‘Between 8 April 2024 and 8 February 2025’. On the 8 April
     reading, whether a March 2025 instalment was missed decides whether Rocky’s oral undertaking (to ‘ensure … all payments’)
     is already breached. The closing paragraph speaks only of ‘any amount when due’ (+15, hedged under R-M).
  9. II.F1 is never stated. The $10,000 was to be paid ‘to me directly’, so Rocky is the promisee, and privity is no bar to his claim
     against whoever promised (Coulls 494). It needs one line (+12).
 10. Gibbs J in Godecke (646–7) is dictum. ‘Doubted’ is a fair verb, but the answer then relies on it as the decisive reason (‘so on
     balance’). A strict marker would want ‘in obiter’ (+2), as the sheet’s D3 entry for Godecke 646 to 647 shows.
 11. Pierre’s ‘expected’ is labelled an ‘invitation to treat’. That is defensible, because it drew Rocky’s offer, but ‘a request, not
     an offer’ is the safer course term and costs the same.
 12. Pao On conditions are stated incompletely. The answer gives the request and ‘was to be remunerated’, but omits that the
     payment ‘must have been legally enforceable had it been promised in advance’. That is harmless on these facts but incomplete
     (+8).
 13. The lender’s opening act is not named on the facts in II.G. ‘Post-contractual conduct cannot interpret the term’ is stated
     without the fact it excludes: Grace’s ‘appropriate monthly repayments’ from 8 April 2024 (and his own ‘I have until 8 April
     2025’). Name them (+10). A reader should not have to infer which conduct is meant.
 14. Footnote style is inconsistent. Judges are named in every footnote except Masters (fn 6: Dixon CJ, McTiernan and Kitto JJ)
     and Australian Woollen Mills (fn 31 and 36: the Court). Either name them or adopt one convention.

4. Is this the objectively best answer?

No. It is a very good answer, accurate at source to a degree few scripts reach, and its reasoning on the 12 February email, Meehan
and illusoriness, and privity is close to the best that 1,500 words allow. It is not the best answer for five reasons:

  1. Part I answers the wrong claimant’s question first. It defends against Grace instead of testing Rocky’s own best claim,
     that AMPL’s advance at his request was his lending.
  2. Three points that matter to Rocky’s exposure are missing. The letter-of-comfort argument against his being bound by
     ‘ensure’ is not made. The Pao On third-party answer on consideration is absent. The duty to co-operate is not put on ‘once
     Grace repays’.
  3. Two authorities are stretched beyond what they decide. Australian Woollen Mills is used for a bilateral promise. Lord
     Reid’s carrier and stevedore requisites are used for whether a director binds his company.
  4. One narrated fact is downgraded to a party’s assumption (C1), and Part I and Part II disagree on the guarantee (C3).
  5. The March instalment and the further information about whom Pierre spoke for are not used. Both decide parts of
     the bottom line.
