I. KIM’S CLAIM AGAINST QUEENBEA

A. Formation

The advertisement gives Kim no right to ‘$500 per post’. A website proposal invites offers unless it ‘clearly indicates’ an intention to be bound,[1] and ‘Apply through the link below’ seeks applications, like the invitation ‘to make formal application’ in Gibson.[2] Decisively, Kim saw it only after the Festival, so could not have accepted it.[3]

Intention to create legal relations is likely present, though Kim bears the onus.[4] QueenBea may point to the ‘third round of drinks’ between friends who laughed at the sudden formality. Yet the test is what ‘would objectively be conveyed by what was said or done’,[5] and paid promotion of a commercial festival by a ‘prominent’ influencer, on standard forms, conveys legal commitment. Decisively, Kim signed and emailed the form three days later and both performed, conduct admissible to discover that intention.[6]

Kim’s signed form was an offer to which Bea, who ‘read it quickly and put it in her bag’, manifested no assent.[7] Kim having proposed that ‘we can write it down on yours’, Bea’s signed form was a counter-offer operating, in words Lawton LJ adopted, ‘to kill the original offer’.[8] Kim accepted it by signing and emailing ‘this all looks great to me’.

Acceptance likely took effect on 31 August, when the email became ‘capable of being retrieved’ at an address Bea designated.[9] Bea will say a business card is a ‘mere indication’ of an address, so receipt awaited her awareness.[10] Yet an offer that ‘expressly specifies’ an address designates it,[11] as Bea did in asking for the agreement at the card’s address. Otherwise, Kim’s performance was arguably acceptance by conduct,[12] though placing her hashtag first makes it equivocal.

QueenBea’s best answer is Rob’s missing signature: Bea pencilled ‘Kays’ and ‘Rob Kay’ onto the form, nodded when told Rob would sign, and asked for the ‘finalised agreement’. Whether a contract awaits every signature ‘depends upon the intention disclosed by the language’,[13] and the form imposed every obligation on Kim and none on Rob, so his signature could only make him a party. Bea’s payment for ‘images which complied with the agreement’ confirms formation, being admissible post-contractual conduct.[14] A contract between QueenBea and Kim was therefore likely formed, the offer to Rob remaining unaccepted.

B. The hashtag order

Butler is analogous: the sellers’ conditions ‘had disappeared from the story’ once they signed the buyers’ acknowledgment,[15] and Kim’s email never mentioned her own form, so printed clause 4, with the QueenBea hashtag ‘listed first’, governs. Kim’s strongest counter is that Bea, having read Kim’s ‘condition’ that #valYOUable ‘must appear first’, said ‘you can include it in all posts’, objectively assenting to it. Alternatively, Lord Denning MR might scrap irreconcilable terms,[16] leaving no order requirement. Yet Bea read Kim’s form only ‘quickly’, and her words address inclusion, not order, beside a printed clause settling order expressly. Lord Denning MR’s view was his alone; Lawton LJ applied ‘set rules’,[17] under which Kim’s unqualified signature accepted clause 4.

The six posts of 1 to 3 September, carrying ‘#valYOUable and #empowHERment hashtags, in that order’, therefore likely breached clause 4 and, by clause 7, earned nothing, though Bea’s remark keeps this arguable. Waiver, and restitution for unpaid posts, are outside scope.

C. The 4 September posts

Clause 5’s proviso does not make Kim’s promise illusory, since it leaves her only ‘a latitude of choice as to the manner’ of performance.[18] On whether she validly invoked it, Meehan has no majority: Gibbs CJ required only honest satisfaction, ‘not that of some hypothetical reasonable man’;[19] Mason J and Wilson J left reasonableness open, though both read the authorities as favouring honesty.[20]

Firstly, Kim was plainly honest: she ‘looked in the mirror’ and was ‘looking incredibly tired’. Secondly, however, if reasonableness applies, Bea will say tiredness is not unfitness, since Kim was fit to attend, and that her face was what QueenBea bought. Yet Bea pencilled the proviso subjectively, turning on whether ‘Kim is not satisfied’, and Kim still posted two Festival images with QueenBea’s hashtag first. Meehan is distinguishable in Kim’s favour: there the condition decided whether the purchaser had to complete a land purchase at all,[21] which is why Mason J feared escape ‘on a mere declaration’,[22] whereas clause 5 alters only the manner of one day’s images. The 4 September posts therefore likely complied, though finely balanced if reasonableness applies.

D. The 5 September posts

Clause 2 required each image to ‘remain visible for the duration of the Festival’, so deleting the 5 September posts after a full day’s visibility breached it unless the 3 September concession binds. QueenBea will say Kim gave nothing, since a promise ‘to do no more than the promisor is bound to do’ is no consideration,[23] and this was no bona fide compromise, as Kim never asserted she was not bound.[24]

Kim’s answer is practical benefit, as recast in Musumeci.[25] Firstly, Kim had contracted to supply services for payment. Secondly, her ‘thinking about discontinuing’ gave Bea reason to doubt completion. Thirdly, Bea promised an ‘other concession (such as reducing A’s original obligation)’,[26] cutting visibility to a day. Fourthly, Bea ‘knew it was unlikely that she could find a replacement at such short notice’, so, as in Roffey, she avoided ‘engaging other people’.[27] Four more days of posts were therefore ‘capable of being viewed by B as worth more to B than any likely remedy against A’.[28] Kim, too, risked her other sponsorships by staying, as the Musumeci tenants stayed ‘rather than walking away at the cost of damages’.[29]

The strongest counter is unfair pressure: Glidewell LJ’s paradigm is a subcontractor who ‘declines to continue with it unless’ paid more.[30] Yet Kim demanded nothing, her reason was genuine, and Bea proposed the concession; one ‘extended by B, without extortion’ supports an inference of consideration.[31] Nor was it ‘expressly limited to a period of difficult circumstances’,[32] its only condition being a day’s visibility. The better view is that the variation binds, so clause 7 does not bite, though Musumeci is a first-instance decision; promissory estoppel, the fallback, is outside scope.

E. The fee

Nothing limited Best Power’s review, it revised the draft, and nothing records signing; while a contract ‘involving the possibility of new terms’ remains to be adopted, the parties are still negotiating.[142] The fourth class fails too, since New Auto, having said it did ‘need to ensure’ supply, was never content to be bound ‘exclusively’ without it. Nor does the absence of subject to contract words matter, since ‘no special form of words is essential’.[143]

New Auto, like Best Power, was therefore likely free to withdraw on 18 July, though this is finely balanced given the minor edits. On the first, second or fourth class, New Auto was bound to buy 49% for A$10 million, subject to the conditions below.

C. The buyer

Only a party may enforce a contract, and party status turns on the instrument itself.[144] Any contract formed on 27 June is New Auto’s, as offeror; but if one arises only on execution of an agreement naming New Auto Australia Pty Ltd as buyer, as James proposed, Best Power must sue the subsidiary, and New Auto, a stranger, could not enforce any supply promise ‘for New Auto’.

D. The conditions

If New Auto was bound, its finance condition was neither uncertain nor illusory: New Auto judges satisfaction honestly, for Gibbs CJ, and perhaps also reasonably, for Mason J,[145] so it could not invoke finance to escape a supply grievance. Mason J let each party avoid because the Meehan clause made the contract ‘null and void’;[146] New Auto’s clause lacks those words, so the right to avoid may be New Auto’s alone.

Were the supply words a condition rather than a promise, they would be certain, like the Meehan condition meant to ensure ‘a sufficient supply of crude oil’,[147] and Sunny’s refusal would let New Auto honestly treat them as unmet; but, unlike the finance term, they carry no ‘subject to’.

Sunny should therefore be advised that Best Power likely cannot enforce the sale.

II. NEW AUTO’S CLAIM AGAINST BEST POWER

New Auto’s strongest argument is that its supply requirement sat in the very email Sunny answered with ‘sounds good’. Even if promissory, it fixes no quantity, price or delivery. New Auto therefore has no supply claim.

A. The Engagement Letter

James’s strongest argument is that he never returned the signed copy requested and stayed silent for 12 days, and silence is generally not acceptance.[152] Yet an offeree with ‘a reasonable opportunity to reject’ services who takes their benefit, in circumstances indicating payment according to the offer, may be held to accept.[153] Empirnall is closely analogous: the developer never signed the contract sent for signature, kept taking the work and was bound, because its objection went only ‘to the manner of acknowledging’ the terms.[154] Here James, on 28 June, said ‘well done. Please proceed with the other work’, a request stronger than Empirnall’s continued receipt of work. His private view that the fee was ‘a bit high’ is irrelevant.[155] A contract on the letter’s terms was likely formed on 28 June.

Stage 1 preceded acceptance, a point Empirnall left undecided,[156] but the Pao On conditions are met: New Auto requested it, ‘Can you please let us know your fee?’ shows both parties expected payment, and a promise to pay for requested legal work would have been enforceable in advance.[157] Nathan is entitled to A$30,000, now paid.

Because Nathan promised only to ‘try’, James’s promise was accepted by delivery of the draft, which was ‘also an executed consideration’.[158] A promise to perform an existing duty is no consideration, at least where one contracting party promises the other ‘to do no more than the promisor is bound to do’.[159]

PART 1: CAROL DANVERS

I. CAROL’S CLAIM AGAINST NICK

A. Formation at the meeting

Nick’s ‘$100,000’ rejected Carol’s opening fee, and her ‘$200,000’ subject to ‘satisfactory materials’ was a counter-offer.[261] Nick’s ‘Okay but I don’t do anything unless it’s in writing’ added a stipulation, so on the majority’s analysis it too was a counter-offer, which Carol’s ‘Great’ accepted.[262] Under Masters v Cameron, a contract binds if the writing only restates settled terms (the first class) or conditions performance (the second), but not if the parties intend no ‘concluded bargain at all’ until it is signed (the third).[263] Intention to create legal relations, ordinarily present in business, turns on the same stipulation.[264]

For Carol, firstly, as the Court said in Masters, ‘All the essentials of a contract are there’: the holograms, price, deadline and her condition were settled, and Nick said ‘Okay’.[265] Secondly, Nick used no formula such as ‘subject to contract’, so ‘it becomes a question of construction’,[266] and a stipulation for a formal agreement ‘does not, by itself, show that they continue merely in negotiation’.[267] Thirdly, ‘I don’t do anything’ speaks of Nick’s performance, fitting the second class, where, as Rich and Starke JJ held in Niesmann, payment due on signing made signing ‘a condition of the obligation to pay’.[268] Fourthly, Carol promptly engaged Monica, and post-contractual conduct is admissible on formation.[269]

The strongest counter is that writing ‘for my lawyers to look at’ is of similar import to ‘subject to contract’, words which ‘prima facie create an overriding condition’.[270] Masters itself turned on solicitors who could add ‘whatever else the solicitors may fairly consider appropriate’.[271] Carol’s ‘standard contract’ may likewise add terms, and Nick’s silence and resort to Dar-Benn point against a contract. Godecke v Kirwan, where a vendor’s solicitors could add terms yet a contract bound, is distinguishable: Walsh J (Mason J agreeing) confined them to consistent terms reasonable ‘in an objective sense’, whereas nothing confined Nick’s lawyers.[272] Carol’s conduct cannot displace ‘the intention disclosed by the language the parties have employed’.[273]

On balance, the better view is the third class: no contract was made, and Nick, who signed nothing, could ‘withdraw at any time until the formal document is signed’.[274] The second class remains arguable; on that view, Nick was bound to join in executing the writing and pay $200,000, so his ‘Good bye!’ was a refusal to perform.[275]

B. The materials condition

If a contract was formed, Nick may call ‘satisfactory’ uncertain or illusory, as a promisor with ‘a discretion or option as to whether he will carry out’ a promise makes no contract.[276] Yet in Meehan v Jones all four judges upheld a condition of finance satisfactory to the purchaser.[277] Gibbs CJ found ‘nothing uncertain’ in terms satisfying the protected party, here Carol, and held that a discretion over fulfilling a condition, not over performance, is not illusory.[278] For Mason J (Wilson J agreeing), the purchaser’s judgment ‘is not an unfettered discretion’ but must be honest, or honest and reasonable, a choice he left undecided.[279] Murphy J added that the protected party may waive it.[280] Since the holograms were built, Carol found satisfactory materials or waived the condition.

C. The emailed contract

On the third class, Carol’s emailed ‘standard contract’ was at most an offer, and silent acceptance is ‘generally insufficient’.[281] Empirnall, where a developer that never signed was bound, turned on ‘taking the benefit of an offer with knowledge of its terms’, and Nick took nothing.[282] An email to an address found on LinkedIn is probably not received until Nick ‘has become aware’ that it was sent there.[283]

Further information is needed on whether Nick contracted for EPEE, since the parties must be identified; if so, Carol’s claim may lie against EPEE.[284] Carol therefore likely has no contractual claim and bears the $100,000 paid to Monica, or $150,000 if Monica enforces the extra $50,000; on the second class, she sues on a $200,000 contract.

PART 2: KAMALA KHAN

II. KAMALA’S CLAIM AGAINST CAROL

A. Party status

Only a party may sue on a contract, as even the dissenting judges in Coulls accepted.[285] Carol’s promise of the extra $50,000 answered Monica’s proposal and was payable ‘once I deliver the holograms’.

For Kamala, firstly, a promise made in her presence about her work and pay may have been made to her and Monica jointly, resting on consideration ‘given on behalf of them all’, as Windeyer J (dissenting) held and Taylor and Owen JJ conditionally accepted.[286] Secondly, by agreeing that Monica would ‘bring in Kamala to get it done’, Carol arguably requested Kamala’s work by implication.[287] If, as planned, Kamala did the work knowing of the promise, that ‘establishes prima facie an acceptance’, and her work would be consideration even if she owed it to Monica, a third party.[288] Thirdly, Windeyer J read the promisee’s ‘I authorise’ in Coulls as ‘equivalent to a requirement’, so ‘You can pay’ could make payment to Kamala a term.[289]

Decisively, however, Carol and Monica met Kamala’s ‘we’re a team!’ with ‘We’re not a team’ before Carol agreed, a reply that objectively excluded Kamala from the bargain.[290] Carol’s promise was offered for Monica’s delivery, not as the price of Kamala’s work, which was Monica’s means of performing, and a request alone does not establish a contract.[291] Kamala is then like C in Mason CJ and Wilson J’s illustration in Trident: where A promises B and C to pay C if B erects a gate, C, though a party, is ‘a stranger to the consideration’.[292] Windeyer J’s reading of ‘authorise’ was a dissent; the majority treated it as a mandate.[293] Carol’s ‘I don’t even know who you are’ is immaterial. Kamala therefore very likely cannot herself sue Carol.

B. Consideration for the extra $50,000

Between Carol and Monica, intention to create legal relations is likely despite the family tie: presumptions are of doubtful utility, Monica is ‘an engineer who specialises in holography’, and Carol paid her from business funds.[294] If their original deal was not contractual, Monica owed no existing duty, but the same family facts would then undermine the $50,000 promise.

On every route through Monica, Carol’s defence is that promising to perform an existing contractual duty to the promisee is no consideration.[295] Monica may answer that the $100,000 bought only the act of ‘developing the two holograms’, so that, unlike the crew in Stilk, she was ‘at liberty to quit’.[296] Yet her ‘I can’t complete the work in time for the current fee’ likely treats timely completion as her undertaking.

The stronger answer is practical benefit, as Musumeci v Winadell recast Williams v Roffey.[297] Monica’s words gave Carol reason to doubt completion, and by promising more Carol obtained the holograms on time and obviated the risk that Nick would sue her, as Roffey sought to avoid a penalty for delay.[298] Monica’s performance was ‘capable of being viewed’ by Carol as worth more than any likely remedy against her.[299] If Nick was never bound, the feared suit was illusory, but Carol still obtained in practice the timely holograms any deal with Nick required.

The strongest counter is element (v), Santow J’s fifth condition, which excludes a promise induced by ‘unfair pressure’.[300] Monica resembles Glidewell LJ’s subcontractor who ‘declines to continue’ unless paid more and may thereby take unfair advantage.[301] Unlike Roffey, whose own surveyor persuaded it to pay, Monica initiated the increase a week before delivery, and Carol agreed only after ‘some consternation’.[302] Yet Monica, whose task proved ‘even more complicated to create than she expected’, stated an inability and offered a solution. Unforeseen difficulty does not ‘justify a demand for re-negotiation backed by unfair pressure’, but may influence fairness through the proportionality of any pressure.[303] On balance, practical benefit likely supplies consideration, though element (v) is finely balanced.

C. Enforcement through Monica

On Deane J’s view alone in Trident, a trust intention ‘should be inferred if it clearly appears’ that the promisee intended the third party to be entitled to insist on performance, and if trust is the appropriate mechanism.[304] ‘You can pay’ reads, like Arthur Coulls’s ‘authorise’, as Monica’s revocable direction, not Kamala’s right to insist, so a trust is unlikely.[305]

Otherwise, Monica may sue. Windeyer J, dissenting, would, ‘as at present advised’, order a promisor to perform a promise to pay a third person, here Kamala.[306] He also rejected the view that the promisee’s damages are only nominal, giving examples such as a third party she owes or feels bound to recompense, but those damages, which could be ‘less, or more’ than $50,000, would be Monica’s own.[307] Assuming no trust and a wholly gratuitous arrangement, he was ‘not persuaded’ that the third party could force the promisee to sue.[308] A trust or Monica’s suit could thus yield Kamala $50,000.

III. KAMALA’S CLAIM AGAINST MONICA

Further information is needed on whether Monica promised to pay Kamala for the work; if so, Kamala can likely recover the promised sum.[309] If Kamala, who ‘has always dreamed of working with’ Carol, helped without any promise, she has no claim.
