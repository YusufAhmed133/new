Foakes v Beer (1884) 9 App Cas 605

• Foakes owed Beer     2,090 19s
• Agreement to pay by instalments
   – 500 up front
   – 150 each month until all paid
   – Beer agreed not to sue for the money awarded to her in the judgement
   – No mention in judgment of interest payable
      • but: ‘an acquittance under seal [ie DEED], in full satisfaction of the whole,
         would have been valid and binding’
• Foakes paid sum due in full over time
• Beer sued for interest
• House of Lords agreed that interest was payable
   – Foakes had not provided any consideration for Beer’s promise
     not to sue.
   – Foakes had only agreed to meet his existing legal duty to pay the
     debt as owed.
                 Existing Legal Duty Rule

TA Sundell & Sons Pty Ltd v Emm Yannoulatos
  (Overseas) Pty Ltd (1955) 56 SR (NSW) 323
The general rule is that a promise to perform an existing duty is
  not consideration, at least:
• when the promise is made by a party to a pre-existing
  contract,
• when it is made to the promisee under that contract, and
• it is to do no more than the promisor is bound to do under
  that contract. (Wigan v Edwards (1973) 1 ALR 497, 512 (Mason
  J))

Often arises in cases of a variation to the terms of an agreement
General rule

• A promise to perform an existing contractual duty is not
   regarded as sufficient consideration


3 exceptions to the rule:

• practical benefit (Williams v Roffey; Musumeci v Winadell)
• promises to third parties (Pao On v Lau Yiu Long – class 2A)
• Genuine/ bona fide compromise (Wigan v Edwards)

Musumeci v Winadell Pty Ltd – Practical Benefit

Santow J: 3 reasons which might be put as to why a contract to
perform an existing obligation should not be enforced.
1. Protect promisor from extortion
       Doctrine for avoiding contracts on the grounds of economic duress is
       not yet fully formed
2. Promisee suffered no legal detriment in performing what
   was already due from him; nor did they receive any legal
   benefit. – but this ignores the fact the promisee might
   receive a benefit even if not a legal benefit.
3. A benefit which is merely the hoped-for end result of
   performance cannot constitute consideration.

Re Selectmove Ltd [1995] 1 WLR 474                                        Consideration: Part 2


•   Selectmove owed tax.
•   Tax Dept petitioned to have Selectmove wound up over the debt.

•   Selectmove argued - a representative of Tax Dept had agreed to allow Selectmove
    pay the tax in arrears. Selectmove contended that this either gave rise to a contract
    or estoppel preventing Inland Revenue from immediately acting on the debt.

•   Inland Revenue argued that the representative had no authority to make any such
    promise. The representative had made this clear to Selectmove by telling them he
    would need to seek permission from his superiors. They also noted that in any case,
    Selectmove had also failed to pay the tax in arrears.


• The UK Ct of Appeal affirmed the principle that a promise to pay an existing
  debt is not consideration. The principle of ‘practical benefit’ in Williams v
  Roffey Bros & Nicholls (Contractors) Ltd did not apply to debt cases.

                         Wigan v Edwards

• 15 April 1969: Edwards agreed to buy a house from Wigan. Contract did
  not contain any term about the house being free from structural defects.
• The Edwards signed the contract.
• The Edwards then had a discussion with Wigan regarding remedial matters
  which required attention. They gave Wigan the list of matters to be
  attended to.
• 22 April 1969: Wigan signed a warranty that he would remedy minor
  defects, and correct any major defects within 5 years.
• Wigan failed to do so.
• The Edwards sued Wigan for damages and obtained $6,000 in the District
  Court of Queensland.
• An appeal to the Full Court of Queensland was dismissed.
• Seller (Wigan) appealed to the HCA.
