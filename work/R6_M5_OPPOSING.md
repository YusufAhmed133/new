### 1.2 Gibbs J in Godecke: 'concluded bargain', 'a vital matter', '[p]erhaps'

Source. Godecke 646 to 647, Gibbs J: 'it seems to me that there would be no binding contract in such a case … the same conclusion might have been reached by holding that there can be no concluded bargain if a vital matter (such as price or rental) has been left to the determination of one of the parties … Perhaps it may be different where agreement has been reached on all essential terms but the determination of subsidiary matters has been left to one of the parties. In Sweet & Maxwell … "such other covenants and conditions as shall be reasonably required" … I am, with respect unable to agree with that observation [Bray CJ in Powell v Jones, upholding a lease without "reasonably"], for it seems to me that the members of the Court of Appeal in Sweet & Maxwell … placed considerable reliance on the fact that the parties had imported the familiar and objective standard of reasonableness … However, it is unnecessary to express any concluded opinion on these matters' (CB14 ch06 p 187).

(verify.py lists 'a vital matter' and 'concluded bargain' first at Meehan 577, where they appear in the vendor's submission. The words also appear verbatim at Godecke 647 in Gibbs J's own judgment, so footnote 16's attribution is correct.)

Fidelity. 'Obiter' is correct. 'Doubted' is a fair gloss on 'might have been reached'. The answer says the '[p]erhaps' 'cited a case where parties had "imported …"'. Gibbs J also cited Powell v Jones, which had no reasonableness qualifier, but only to reject Bray CJ's extension. So the answer's reading, that the 'perhaps' is confined to clauses importing reasonableness, is the better reading and arguably understates the point. Not a defect.

Grace's counsel's strongest reply (partly met). The email 'fixes the amount, date, term and rate', which are the essentials of a loan, as the answer itself says in its first paragraph. Grace would say the 'conditions' are subsidiary matters, so Gibbs J's 'perhaps' applies, and Mason J in Meehan 589 implies 'honestly, or honestly and reasonably' into 'satisfactory'. That would supply the reasonableness standard Gibbs J required. The answer's reply ('which these parties never did') relies on the parties not having imported reasonableness expressly. Implication is Grace's point, and the answer meets it only through the Meehan distinction (see 1.3). The answer never says why the 'conditions' are 'a vital matter' rather than subsidiary ones. A harsh marker could write 'label asserted'. This is contestable. Proposed edit E3 below sharpens the decisive reason so that the label carries less weight.

### 1.3 The Meehan distinction: 'Rocky's covers the loan's own terms, which neither standard can supply and no range confines.'

Grace's counsel's strongest reply (contestable, and the weakest clause in the paragraph). Gibbs CJ in Meehan 579 said that on an objective test a court 'should not find it unduly difficult to decide what finance a reasonable man, in the position of the purchaser, would regard as satisfactory', even though the clause was 'silent as to amount, term of the loan, rate of interest, conditions of repayment …'. So a reasonableness standard can supply loan terms. Pace 151 (McHugh JA) adds that 'even when no objective criterion of measurement is available, it may still be possible to infer a promise to act honestly and/or reasonably'. 'Which neither standard can supply' is therefore open to direct contradiction from Meehan 579. That is the same case the answer distinguishes, and the second passage is on the same Pace page the answer cites at footnote 13.

The decisive reason is a different passage, which the answer half-quotes. Gibbs CJ, Meehan 581: the Placer principle 'does not apply where the discretion or option of the contracting party relates, not to the performance of the contractual obligations themselves, but only to the fulfilment of a condition upon which the contract depends.' Rocky's 'satisfactory conditions' are the terms of his own lending, so they go to the performance of his own obligation. That is the Placer side of Gibbs CJ's line, and it matches Pace 151's Placer example ('a subsidy "of an amount or at a rate determined by the Commonwealth"'). Quoting the other half of Gibbs CJ's sentence makes the distinction from the source's own words and drops the contestable 'neither standard' clause. See edit E3.

### 1.5 'Rocky likely gave consideration, unless he also promised AMPL a guarantee when he "asked".' CERTAIN DEFECT (internal inconsistency)

The paragraph runs: (a) the rule applies 'at least when' there is an existing contract, and none is shown; (b) 'Even otherwise, ensuring "all payments" "promptly" exceeds guaranteeing "repayment of the Loan"'; (c) the closing caveat above. Sentence (b) holds that a prior duty to guarantee 'repayment' leaves fresh consideration, because the 8 April undertaking promised more. A 27 February promise of 'a guarantee' therefore defeats consideration only if it already covered every payment, promptly. As written, the caveat contradicts (b): it says any guarantee promised on 27 February would defeat consideration. Source for the test: Wigan 594 (Mason J): the rule applies where the new promise 'is to do no more than the promisor is bound to do'; Sundell 327: 'every promise allegedly given … was identical with a promise given under the original agreement'.

Fix (E1, saves 1 word): 'unless, when he "asked", he already undertook the same.' 'The same' refers back to 'ensuring "all payments" "promptly"', which is exactly the Sundell or Wigan identity test.

## 6. Authenticity (ignoring the rubric)

It reads as a real lawyer working the problem forward. Each Part opens from the client's question ('decides Rocky's claim to repayment'; 'whether he can claim $10,000 and must pay AMPL'). The facts are taken in date order. Each authority is set beside the facts it decided, and the answer distinguishes cases on their facts: Meehan's finance condition, Godecke's reasonableness, Mrs Coulls' signature, Empirnall's unsigned developer. It has three real strengths:
- textual analysis of the deciding words ('"recorded" equally governs the 4% rate'; 'the words name Grace, whose payment the $10,000 priced');
- the 'right in result but wrong in reason' treatment of Grace's 'I never signed anything with you';
- the closing exposure paragraph, which turns every branch into a sum and a date.

Where it slips:
- Compression in places tips into quote-mosaic. For example, 'ensuring "all payments" "promptly" exceeds guaranteeing "repayment of the Loan"' works, but three quotations in eight words is near the limit. Quoted words are 16.5% of the body.
- Two steps are label-asserted rather than shown: 'a vital matter', and 'supplying that authority'.
- One step in the consideration paragraph contradicts its own previous sentence (1.5).
- One sentence is hard to parse (the Pao On sentence).

None of this is box-ticking. The reasoning is the client's, not a checklist's.
