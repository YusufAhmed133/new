I. ROCKY v GRACE

The first issue is whether the February dealings formed a loan contract, deciding Rocky’s claim to repayment and any claim by Grace that he failed to lend. Grace’s 5 February call asked Rocky to make an offer and promised nothing; it was likely an invitation to treat.[^1] Rocky could say his 12 February email records discussions, its other points, ‘Potential’ and ‘wait and see’, promising nothing. However, ‘I will lend’ fixes the amount, date, term and rate, and the email seeks agreement. Unlike ‘may be prepared to sell’, those words are promissory,[^2] so the first point is likely an offer.

Acceptance requires an external manifestation of assent.[^3] ‘Confirmed’ arguably vouched only for the email’s accuracy; but it answered a request to ‘confirm agreement’ without new terms, so it was no counter-offer and likely accepted the first point, ‘subject to satisfactory conditions’ included. Grace’s implied promise to repay would arguably be consideration, since a promise can be the price of a promise.[^4]

The qualifier raises whether this preliminary agreement showed an intention to create legal relations. That is judged objectively, the alleging party bearing the onus;[^5] the parties’ status as ‘project developers’ favours intention. As nothing was made ‘subject to a formal contract’, the question is whether the terms were to ‘be put into form’ or left to ‘a new agreement’.[^6] Grace would say the fixed terms show the parties had ‘reached finality’, Masters’ first class.[^7] Rocky would invoke the third class, where parties ‘have dealt only with major matters’;[^8] though no formal document was contemplated, so did the email. Its ‘conditions’, unlike ‘subject to success of Hail Mary Project’, name no external event, so they are most naturally further loan terms; and ‘satisfactory’ leaves them to the protected party’s judgment,[^9] here Rocky’s as lender. On balance, the loan was likely left to a new agreement. Were the conditions settled ‘this morning’, the email would bind, but Rocky’s duty would arise only once they were met.[^10]

The qualifier also arguably makes Rocky’s promise illusory, since a consideration is illusory if it ‘depends upon an unfettered discretion vested in the promisor’.[^11] Grace may reply that, as in Meehan, Rocky must judge honestly, perhaps reasonably, and that the fixed terms are ‘specified parameters’.[^12] However, Meehan is distinguishable: its discretion related ‘only to the fulfilment of a condition’, finance, within agreed terms;[^13] Rocky’s covers the loan’s own terms, which neither standard can supply and no range confines. Godecke bound because its added terms had to be ‘reasonable in an objective sense’, and Gibbs J’s obiter ‘[p]erhaps’, for subsidiary matters once ‘all essential terms’ are agreed, likewise assumed ‘the familiar and objective standard of reasonableness’,[^14] which these parties never imported. The email therefore likely bound neither party, defeating Grace’s claim.

Rocky may instead claim that AMPL’s advance, made because he ‘asked’, performed his promise, so Grace owes him. Even had the email bound, the advance differed in amount, date, lender and rate. As Pierre ‘agreed to provide the money’ and AMPL named Rocky guarantor, which no lender acting for him would need, AMPL did not contract ‘as agent’ for him.[^15] Rocky’s 5 March demand under the Loan Agreement, arguably assuming a 27 February start, faces privity: ‘a third party cannot sue upon a contract’, nor can ‘a stranger to the consideration’.[^16] Party status turns on ‘the written instrument itself’,[^17] and Grace’s promises were made to AMPL, not Rocky, even as joint promisee;[^18] nor did Rocky give Grace anything, AMPL having lent. Grace’s ‘I never signed anything with you’ is right in result but wrong in reason: signature is not necessary, Empirnall being bound unsigned,[^19] nor decisive, Mrs Coulls signing without becoming a party.[^20]

Thus Rocky has no present claim against Grace. The Development Agreement repays him first only ‘if the Project made a profit’, none yet shown; and Grace never promised to reimburse what he pays AMPL.

II. ROCKY v AMPL (ALTERNATIVELY PIERRE)

Rocky’s questions are whether he can claim $10,000 and must pay AMPL. On offer and acceptance at the 8 April meeting, Pierre’s expressed expectation promised nothing, so it was likely an invitation to treat.[^21] Rocky’s ‘I will do that if you also promise’ offered a bilateral contract,[^22] which Pierre accepted when he ‘agreed’.[^23] AMPL may argue that nothing bound before signing; but that ‘depends upon the intention disclosed by the language’ used,[^24] and Rocky reserved no right to withdraw pending a document signed only by Grace and AMPL, so a contract likely formed orally.

Who promised turns on agency: Pierre bound AMPL if, with its consent, he contracted ‘as agent’ for it rather than solely for himself.[^25] Rocky’s ‘you’ points to Pierre; but Pierre controls AMPL, had agreed to its 27 February advance, and spoke at its office about its loan, so he likely acted for AMPL. If he said he would pay personally, he alone owes the $10,000, and AMPL cannot sue on the undertaking.

On consideration, AMPL may invoke the existing legal duty rule: ‘also’ arguably presupposes a guarantee already given, so, as in Sundell, promising to ensure performance already owed adds nothing.[^26] However, the rule applies ‘at least when’ the promise is made to the promisee under an existing contract,[^27] and none existed: on 27 February Rocky only ‘asked’, and the clause, arguably included after Pierre ‘agreed’, bound no one before signing. Even otherwise, ensuring ‘all payments’ ‘promptly’ exceeds guaranteeing ‘repayment of the Loan’; and if Pierre promised, the exception for promises to third parties applies, any duty running to AMPL.[^28] Rocky likely gave consideration unless the clause preceded the bargain.

On intention to create legal relations, Pierre is Rocky’s cousin, but presumptions at most identify who bears the onus,[^29] here Rocky; and a fixed sum promised at AMPL’s office as its loan was finalised is ‘appropriately promissory in character’.[^30] Nor is Rocky’s undertaking a mere letter of comfort: Rogers CJ saw ‘no relevant difference’ between a ‘practice to ensure’ that a borrower could repay and a promise ‘to ensure’ it.[^31] Rocky’s words were plainer and priced, so both promises likely bind.

On certainty, the Loan Agreement fixes the payments Rocky must ‘ensure’, so his undertaking is not ‘so obscure’ as to be void.[^32] His ‘I will do that’ contains no words of effort: it likely promised that Grace would pay, not that Rocky would try. AMPL’s promise is not illusory either: once Grace repays, it leaves ‘no discretion’.[^33]

On privity, the $10,000 was to be paid ‘to me directly’ for consideration Rocky himself gave: he is its promisee, no stranger to the consideration.[^34] ‘[T]he clause’ most naturally means the guarantee clause; either way, Rocky relies on the oral promise.

Rocky’s exposure turns next on whether the guarantee clause binds him. A guarantee was ‘provided by Rocky’, possibly only by his oral undertaking, and only ‘the parties to it’ are ‘bound by the obligations of a contract’.[^35] Silence while a clause is included is generally not acceptance.[^36] AMPL would say Rocky ‘attended’ as the clause was included, taking the $10,000 promise knowing its terms,[^37] and later invoked the Loan Agreement. However, the benefit he took, AMPL’s promise, priced his own words, and nothing shows ‘a reasonable opportunity to reject’ the clause. He is likely bound by his oral undertaking, and by the clause only if he saw its words.

AMPL must also show consideration moving from itself,[^38] which its $10,000 promise supplies if AMPL made it. Otherwise its advance is past consideration,[^39] counting only if requested, understood to be remunerated, and enforceable if promised in advance; Rocky ‘asked’, but unlike Pao On, where a return was understood ‘at the time of the main agreement’,[^40] nothing shows a guarantee was then contemplated. AMPL might add that it signed after the clause’s inclusion, arguably ‘really done in consideration of’ the guarantee;[^41] but Rocky priced his undertaking at $10,000, not at AMPL’s signature, so the clause may lack consideration.

Whether the 9 March call was premature depends on when the 12 months began. Construction is objective, and in New South Wales post-contractual conduct, such as Grace’s interest payments or ‘I have until 8 April 2025’, cannot construe the term;[^42] but, if it is ambiguous, mutually known facts showing ‘the genesis of the transaction’ are admissible.[^43] Naming no start, it is arguably ambiguous. AMPL argues it ran from the 27 February advance, which the agreement ‘recorded’; but ‘recorded’ equally governs the 4% rate, which nothing shows predated 8 April, and the genesis shows only when money moved. The term therefore arguably ran from 8 April 2024, making the call premature, unless the Loan Agreement says otherwise. Interest is shown paid only to 8 February 2025; if the March instalment went unpaid, Rocky’s undertaking is arguably broken.

The $10,000 is payable ‘once Grace repays the Loan’, a condition precedent to that obligation, not to the contract, which bound at once:[^44] nothing is yet payable. Rocky could argue that, read commercially, his own payment satisfies it;[^45] but the words name Grace, and the $10,000 priced his promise that Grace would pay. Nor does AMPL breach the duty to co-operate, to ‘do everything necessary on [its] part’:[^46] its call does not stop Grace repaying.

Accordingly, if Rocky assented to the clause, he is liable for $1,150,000 once the Loan is due and unpaid: now, on a 27 February start; otherwise from 8 April 2025. If bound only orally, he is liable in damages, to AMPL or Pierre, for any payment Grace misses. No term requires him to pay early, and paying likely forfeits the $10,000, payable only if Grace, who said he ‘might not pay at all’, repays.
