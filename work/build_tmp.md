**I. ROCKY v GRACE**

The first issue is whether the February dealings formed a loan contract, which decides both Rocky’s claim to repayment and any claim by Grace that he failed to lend ‘by 15 February’. Grace’s 5 February call asked Rocky to make an offer and promised nothing, so it was likely an invitation to treat,[^1] which Rocky declined. Rocky could argue his 12 February email merely records the meeting, as it opens ‘Confirming discussions’ and its other points promise nothing. However, ‘I will lend’ fixes the amount, date, term and rate, and ‘Please confirm agreement’ seeks assent; unlike ‘may be prepared to sell’,[^2] these words are promissory, so the first point is likely an offer, though ‘subject to satisfactory conditions’.

Acceptance requires ‘an external manifestation of assent to an offer’.[^3] ‘Confirmed’ arguably vouched only for the email’s accuracy; however, it answered a request to ‘confirm agreement’ and added no terms, so it was no counter-offer and likely accepted the first point, qualifier included. Grace’s implied promise to repay would arguably be consideration, as the price of a promise ‘can be in the form of an act, forbearance or promise’.[^4]

The qualifier raises whether this preliminary agreement showed an intention to create legal relations, judged objectively, the onus lying on the party alleging the contract, though their status as ‘project developers’ favours it.[^5] Where nothing is ‘expressly stated to be subject to a formal contract’, the question is whether the terms were to ‘be put into form’ or were ‘subject to a new agreement the terms of which are not expressed in detail’.[^6] Grace would say they had ‘reached finality in arranging all the terms of their bargain’.[^7] However, unlike ‘subject to success of Hail Mary Project’, the ‘conditions’ name no external event, so they are most naturally further loan terms, and ‘satisfactory’ leaves their judgment to the party the condition protects,[^8] here Rocky as lender. Gibbs J, in obiter, doubted that leaving ‘further terms to be settled by one of the parties’ binds, while allowing that it ‘may be different’ for ‘subsidiary matters’;[^9] but he tied that to ‘the familiar and objective standard of reasonableness’,[^10] which ‘satisfactory’ lacks. Later conduct, admissible on formation,[^11] agrees: Rocky never lent, Grace never demanded it, and AMPL supplied the money. On balance, the loan was likely left to a new agreement, unless the conditions were settled ‘this morning’.

The qualifier also arguably makes Rocky’s promise illusory, since a promisor with ‘a discretion or option as to whether he will carry out’ the promise makes ‘no contract on which an action can be brought at all’.[^12] Grace may reply that, as in *Meehan*, Rocky must decide honestly;[^13] however, that saved a discretion relating ‘only to the fulfilment of a condition’ within ‘a concluded agreement as to the terms’,[^14] whereas Rocky’s covers the loan’s own terms, which honesty cannot supply. The email therefore likely bound neither party, defeating any claim that Rocky failed to lend.

Rocky may instead claim that AMPL’s advance, made because he ‘asked’, performed his promise, so Grace owes him. Even had the email bound, the advance differs: $1,150,000, after 15 February, ‘advanced by AMPL’ and ‘recorded’ at 4%. Although a principal is a party where another ‘contracts as agent’ for him,[^15] Pierre ‘agreed to provide the money’ and AMPL named Rocky guarantor, which no lender acting for him would need, so AMPL was not his agent. Rocky’s 5 March demand under ‘the Loan Agreement with AMPL’ therefore meets privity, for ‘a third party cannot sue upon a contract’.[^16] Party status turns on ‘the written instrument itself’,[^17] and Grace’s promises to repay were made to AMPL, not to Rocky, even as joint promisee;[^18] Grace’s ‘I never signed anything with you’ is right in result, though Mrs Coulls signed yet was not a party.[^19]

Thus Rocky has no present claim against Grace. The Development Agreement repays him first ‘if the Project made a profit’, even a ‘minimal’ one, but no breach is shown, and Grace never promised to reimburse anything Rocky pays AMPL.

**II. ROCKY v AMPL (ALTERNATIVELY PIERRE)**

At the 8 April meeting, Pierre’s ‘expected’ promised nothing, so it was likely an invitation to treat.[^20] Rocky’s ‘I will do that if you also promise’ offered a bilateral contract,[^21] which Pierre accepted when he ‘agreed’.[^22] AMPL may argue that nothing bound before signing, but parties are bound ‘when, and in the manner in which, they intend’, as Lord Greene MR said of exchanged contracts,[^23] and Rocky’s undertaking did not await a document ‘signed by Grace and AMPL’, so a contract likely formed orally.

Who promised turns on what Pierre’s words objectively conveyed. Rocky’s ‘you’ points to Pierre, but Pierre controls AMPL and spoke at its office about its loan, so he likely contracted ‘as agent’ for it.[^24] If Pierre was to pay personally, he alone owes the $10,000, and AMPL, not Rocky’s promisee, cannot sue on his undertaking.

On consideration, AMPL may invoke the existing legal duty rule, arguing Rocky’s promise added nothing to his guarantee.[^25] However, the rule applies ‘at least when’ the promise is ‘made to the promisee under that contract’,[^26] and none then existed: on 27 February Rocky only ‘asked’, and ‘Pierre agreed and the clause was included’ suggests the clause followed the bargain. Even otherwise, ensuring ‘all payments’ ‘promptly’ exceeds guaranteeing ‘repayment of the Loan’. If Pierre promised, the exception for promises to third parties applies, as ‘a promise to perform … a pre-existing contractual obligation to a third party can be valid consideration’.[^27] Rocky likely gave consideration.

On intention to create legal relations, Pierre is Rocky’s cousin, but presumptions do ‘no more than invite attention to identifying the party who bears the onus of proof’,[^28] here Rocky, and a fixed sum promised at AMPL’s office as its loan was finalised is ‘appropriately promissory in character’.[^29] Rocky could call his own undertaking a mere letter of comfort; however, Rogers CJ saw ‘no relevant difference’ between a ‘practice to ensure’ repayment and a promise ‘to ensure’ it,[^30] and Rocky’s ‘I will do that’ was plainer and priced, so both promises likely bind.

On certainty, the Loan Agreement fixes the payments Rocky must ‘ensure’, so his undertaking is not ‘so obscure’ as to be void;[^31] his ‘I will do that’ contains no words of effort, so it likely promised that Grace would pay, not that he would try. AMPL’s promise is not illusory either, because its condition, Grace’s repayment, relates ‘only to the fulfilment of a condition’.[^32]

The $10,000 was to be paid ‘to me directly’ for consideration Rocky himself gave, so he is its promisee and no ‘stranger to the consideration’,[^33] and privity is no bar. ‘[T]he clause’ most naturally means the guarantee clause, the only clause described, and Rocky’s claim rests on the oral promise either way.

Whether the guarantee clause binds Rocky is open: a guarantee was ‘provided by Rocky’, perhaps only by his oral undertaking, and only ‘the parties to it’ are ‘bound by the obligations of a contract’.[^34] Silence while a clause is included is generally not acceptance.[^35] AMPL would say the clause recorded Rocky’s own bargain, taken ‘with knowledge of its terms’,[^36] and that his 5 March demand invoked ‘the Loan Agreement’; but Rocky has received nothing, and nothing shows he saw it. He is therefore likely bound by his oral undertaking and arguably by the clause, which evidence that he read it would settle.

AMPL must show consideration moving from itself,[^37] which its $10,000 promise supplies, if AMPL made it. Otherwise its earlier advance is past consideration,[^38] counting only if requested, understood to be ‘remunerated’, and ‘legally enforceable had it been promised in advance’;[^39] Rocky ‘asked’, but nothing shows a guarantee was then contemplated. AMPL’s better answer is that it signed only after the clause was included, arguably ‘really done in consideration of’ the guarantee.[^40]

Whether the 9 March 2025 call was premature depends on when the 12 months began. Post-contractual conduct, such as Grace’s ‘I have until 8 April 2025’, cannot construe the term in New South Wales,[^41] but, the term naming no start, mutually known facts showing ‘the genesis of the transaction’ are admissible.[^42] AMPL argues the term ran from the 27 February advance, perhaps adding that the agreement ‘recorded’ a loan already made; however, ‘recorded’ equally governs the 4% rate, which nothing shows predated 8 April, and the genesis shows only when the money moved. The term therefore arguably ran from 8 April 2024, making the call premature. Interest is shown paid only to 8 February 2025; if the March 2025 instalment was unpaid, Rocky’s undertaking that Grace make ‘all payments’ ‘promptly’ is arguably already broken.

The $10,000 is payable ‘once Grace repays the Loan’, a condition precedent to that obligation rather than to the contract, which bound immediately,[^43] so nothing is yet payable. Rocky could argue that, read commercially, his own payment satisfies it,[^44] but the words name Grace, and the $10,000 priced his promise that Grace would pay. Nor does AMPL breach the duty to co-operate, to ‘do everything necessary on [its] part’,[^45] since nothing suggests its call stops Grace repaying.

Accordingly, if Rocky assented to the clause, he is liable for $1,150,000 once the Loan is due and unpaid: now, if the term ran from 27 February, otherwise from 8 April 2025. If bound only orally, he is liable in damages, to AMPL or else Pierre, for any payment Grace fails to make when due. No term requires him to pay early, and paying himself likely forfeits the $10,000, which falls due only if Grace, who said he ‘might not pay at all’, repays.
